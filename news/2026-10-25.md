## 1. Pennsylvania will require labels on AI-generated ads

### Overview

Pennsylvania has enacted new legislation requiring disclosures on artificial intelligence advertisements.
Governor Josh Shapiro signed the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act into law on Thursday.
The statute targets any advertisement significantly modified or generated by artificial intelligence.
Pennsylvania is now one of the first states to require formal disclosures on AI content.
The mandate requires labels to be delivered in the same format as the advertisement itself.
Regulations apply across multiple media formats, including audio, video, still images, and text.
Both multi-billion-dollar corporate campaigns and small local flyers fall within the scope of the bill.
Legal analysts note that the newly enacted statute contains multiple loopholes and ambiguities.

### What happened

On Thursday, Pennsylvania Governor Josh Shapiro officially signed the new advertising act into law.
The legislation is officially titled the Artificial Intelligence Deceptive Advertising Act.
The bill was formally introduced by Pennsylvania State Senator Nick Pisciottano.
Under the new statute, advertisements using artificial intelligence must include an explicit disclosure.
This rule applies to any advertisement that has been significantly modified or generated by AI.
Disclosures must be provided in the identical format as the advertisement itself.
A video commercial must contain a video disclosure under the statutory rules.
An audio advertisement must feature an accompanying audio disclosure.
The law covers high-profile television commercials produced by multi-billion-dollar companies.
The legislation also applies to small restaurants printing crowded, ugly flyers for wing specials.
The office of State Senator Nick Pisciottano did not immediately return a request for comment.

### Why it matters

Pennsylvania is among the very first states to establish legal disclosure rules for AI media.
The requirement applies strictly regardless of where an advertisement was originally produced.
Out-of-state advertisers must comply if their commercials are expected to air in Pennsylvania.
Viewers in other states may consequently see AI disclosure labels on interstate broadcasts.
A commercial broadcast across several states could require disclosures solely due to Pennsylvania airplay.
The legislation establishes a precedent that could influence artificial intelligence policies across the nation.
The law highlights growing state-level pressure to combat deceptive commercial practices.

### Technical details

The act regulates advertisements across text, audio, video, and still image formats.
Any content significantly modified or generated by artificial intelligence requires disclosure.
Disclosures must match the format of the corresponding advertisement.
The statutory text fails to define what constitutes a significant modification.
The standard for what is significantly modified remains highly subjective under the law.
The act includes explicit statutory carve-outs for parody and satire.
Additional exemptions are granted for commentary and critical expression.
Advertisements deemed evidently fictional are also completely exempt from disclosure rules.
The law provides no clear criteria for determining what qualifies as evidently fictional.
The statutory language indicates disclosures apply only when synthetic content creates a false consumer perception.
The false consumer perception must relate directly to a product or service.
The statute leaves unresolved what specific claims legally qualify as false.
The bill does not specify which regulatory entities will determine whether consumer perception was falsified.

Background:
* Generative artificial intelligence systems can generate synthetic text, audio, images, and video.
* Commercial marketing firms frequently use synthetic tools to reduce creative production costs.
* Consumer protection advocates have raised concerns over misleading synthetic representations of products.
* Transparency legislation often relies on watermarking, metadata, or explicit on-screen consumer notices.
* What is not yet known is how Pennsylvania regulators will define the threshold for significant modification.
* What is not yet known is who will be tasked with enforcing compliance against non-compliant advertisers.
* What is not yet known is the specific penalty structure for violating the disclosure mandate.

### Industry impact

Multi-billion-dollar companies must audit all commercial creative assets airing in Pennsylvania.
Small local businesses must review their promotional print flyers for synthetic content.
Regional television broadcasters must prepare for synthetic media disclosures on commercial feeds.
Creative marketing agencies must navigate the subjective statutory boundary of significant modification.
Advertisers may struggle to determine whether stylized marketing qualifies as evidently fictional.
Marketing legal teams must assess whether AI-generated product images induce a false consumer perception.
Companies running multi-state campaigns may apply universal AI labels to avoid regional compliance friction.
Smaller advertisers with limited legal budgets could face compliance uncertainty under the vague definitions.

### What to watch next

State regulatory agencies may publish clarifying rules regarding the definition of significant modification.
State officials must establish who holds the authority to decide when consumer perception is falsified.
State Senator Nick Pisciottano's office may issue public clarifications on the law's intent.
Observers will monitor how the parody and satire exemptions are applied to real-world campaigns.
Regulatory bodies may clarify how small print flyers will be monitored for compliance.
Other state legislatures could propose similar artificial intelligence disclosure mandates.
Legal challenges may emerge regarding the law's impact on interstate commercial advertising.
Advertisers will watch for the first enforcement actions under the new Pennsylvania statute.

## 2. BlackX UEFI bootkit exploits Baton Drop flaw to bypass Secure Boot

### Overview

A newly discovered UEFI bootkit called BlackX was deployed in an espionage campaign in early 2026.

The bootkit targeted diplomatic agencies located in Central Asia.

It exploited the Baton Drop vulnerability to bypass Secure Boot protections.

Researchers attributed the operation with medium confidence to Chinese state-sponsored threat group APT41.

The bootkit loaded a malicious driver designed to tamper with security tools from Kaspersky and Avast.

Victim endpoints were running fully patched Windows releases but lacked manual Secure Boot revocations.

### What happened

Kaspersky researchers discovered and analyzed the BlackX bootkit in early 2026.

The campaign specifically targeted diplomatic agencies in Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, and Turkmenistan.

Threat analysts attributed the activity with medium confidence to APT41.

APT41 is also tracked under the names Brass Typhoon, Wicked Panda, and Barium.

The operators leveraged the Baton Drop vulnerability, tracked as CVE-2022-21894.

BlackX demonstrated substantial similarities with the BlackLotus bootkit.

BlackLotus has been circulating on underground hacker forums since October 2022.

In March 2023, ESET confirmed that BlackLotus could compromise fully patched Windows 11 systems.

BlackX was deployed to deliver follow-on espionage payloads to compromised diplomatic systems.

### Why it matters

Kaspersky reported that all BlackX victims were running a fully patched Windows release.

The bootkit remained successful because administrators failed to manually trigger necessary revocations.

The official patch for Baton Drop was designed to revoke the Windows Production PCA 2011 certificate.

However, certificate revocation does not happen automatically upon installing the update.

System administrators must manually trigger the revocation process on target hosts.

Kaspersky noted that manual administrator intervention after updating is extremely uncommon.

The threat actors capitalized on this mitigation gap to deploy their copycat bootkit.

The Windows Production PCA 2011 certificate carried an expiration date of October 19, 2026.

Researchers warned that certificate expiration alone does not stop vulnerable binaries from running.

Systems remain susceptible unless administrators explicitly add the certificate to a blocklist.

### Technical details

Kaspersky found BlackX's exploit implementation to be remarkably similar to BlackLotus.

The exploit enables the attacker to enroll custom Machine Owner Keys into firmware.

The bootkit then loads arbitrary EFI files to successfully bypass Secure Boot.

BlackX injects a malicious Windows kernel driver during the boot sequence.

The driver was signed with a certificate issued to Wenjiang District Mingyu Network Technology Service Studio.

The digital certificate used to sign the kernel driver has since been revoked.

The malicious driver disables security-related processes across the operating system.

It actively strips registry callbacks relied upon by endpoint defense solutions.

The driver also tampers with Event Tracing for Windows providers to hinder event monitoring.

Tampering routines were configured to execute only if Kaspersky or Avast software was detected.

Kaspersky confirmed that these interference mechanisms were useless against its own security software.

The driver protects its components by intercepting user attempts to open bootkit files.
