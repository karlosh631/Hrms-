## 1. NY announces game ownership bill requiring ownership rather than licensing

### Overview
New York has joined legislative efforts to protect video game consumers.
Representative Ritchie Torres has introduced the Power To The Players Act.
Torres serves as the representative for New York's 15th congressional district.
The proposed federal legislation targets anti-consumer practices in the gaming market.
Under the proposed bill, buying a video game grants consumer ownership instead of a license.
Publishers would also be required to provide offline functionality before shutting down servers.
The legislation directs the Federal Trade Commission to establish formal game ownership regulations.

### What happened
Representative Ritchie Torres officially introduced the Power To The Players Act.
The bill directs the FTC to establish clear rules regarding video game ownership.
Purchasing a game would legally grant ownership rather than a revocable license.
Torres's announcement aims to curb prevalent anti-consumer business practices.
Games featuring online elements must include an offline mode before server shutdowns occur.
Torres specifically highlighted sports releases such as NBA 2K25 in his announcement.
NBA 2K25 is reportedly removed from digital storefronts with servers ending after two years.
This leaves players unable to access a primary feature of the game they bought.
New York joins Pennsylvania in pushing legislation to safeguard gamers' rights.
Pennsylvania state senator John Kane previously introduced the Protect Our Games Act.
Kane's bill requires publishers to provide an offline mode or community servers.
Alternatively, the Pennsylvania bill mandates a minimum refund of 25 percent.

### Why it matters
Video games are currently more expensive than ever before.
Despite higher prices, games frequently arrive with predetermined expiration dates.
Aggressive in-game monetization schemes have faced intense worldwide player scrutiny.
Consumers expect to enjoy games even after publishers discontinue official support.
Players are pushing back against paying premium prices for temporary access.
Sony has argued that players do not actually own their purchased games.
Sony asserted its legal right to revoke game access whenever it benefits the company.
In an ongoing class-action lawsuit defense, Sony lawyers addressed digital purchases directly.
