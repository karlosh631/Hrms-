## 2. Amazon sends chills through its workforce with mixed message memo about AI use

### Overview

Amazon recently published an engineering treatise addressing developer roles in the age of AI.
The memo was written by Terk Terkowitz, a VP and Distinguished Engineer at the company.
Terkowitz is described as the leader of Amazon's technical contributor career track.
The communication was initially distributed internally to software engineers and managers in September.
Amazon made the treatise public during the final week of October 2026.
The treatise has sent chills through software engineers facing an increasingly bleak career outlook.
It provides contradictory guidance on whether engineers are required to use generative AI tools.
The publication coincides with reports of 250 additional job cuts at Amazon.

### What happened

Terkowitz published a detailed memo exploring how artificial intelligence alters software engineering.
The document attempts to make the future of the software engineering profession feel less bleak.
The memo states that if work does not benefit from AI tooling, that is a finding, not a failing.
Engineers are encouraged to document tool limitations and discuss them directly with their managers.
However, the memo also warns staff that non-adopters of AI tooling are falling behind.
It admits that input metrics are deeply flawed, yet advises managers to use them carefully anyway.
The author notes these three conflicting stances occur within three consecutive sentences.
Many Amazon engineers assume the mixed guidance is connected to ongoing workforce reductions.
Observers interpret the contradictory phrasing as managerial cover for issuing performance improvement plans.

### Why it matters

Many software engineers currently experience profound anxiety regarding their long-term career prospects.
Some tech workers are genuinely questioning whether they should leave the sector for farming.
The upheaval from artificial intelligence is seen as fundamentally dissimilar from prior tech industry transitions.
Conflicting policies allow managers to selectively punish or protect employees based on subjective preference.
A manager can cite a finding when a strong engineer chooses to skip an AI tool.
That same manager can cite falling behind when deciding to place an underperforming worker on a PIP.
When organizations measure raw AI activity, workers inevitably turn those metrics into gaming targets.
The internal directive reveals a stark contradiction in Amazon's overarching cloud business strategy.
Inside Amazon, artificial intelligence token consumption is viewed as an expense that violates frugality.
Outside the company, driving customer token consumption forms the core of Amazon Web Services revenue.

### Technical details

In May, Amazon shut down an internal employee-built tool known as KiroRank.
KiroRank operated as a leaderboard that ranked Amazon workers by their total token usage.
Employees spun up junk tasks simply to climb higher on the KiroRank leaderboard.
This practice of inflating compute metrics was termed tokenmaxxing by workers.
Terkowitz explicitly stated in the memo that tokenmaxxing is directly at odds with frugality.
The treatise emphasized that core engineering judgment and customer outcomes must not be delegated to AI.
Terkowitz highlighted work categories where current artificial intelligence tools offer no actual improvement.
The memo instructed leaders to examine teams in the 90th percentile of AI adoption.
Managers must audit whether heavy token burn actually yields a commensurate increase in software delivery.

### Industry impact

Other major technology companies are grappling with similar employee behavior around generative AI tools.
Meta experienced a comparable issue called Claudenomics among 85,000 employees during the same week in May.
Industry discussions at GitHub Universe revealed that automated agents now initiate roughly 40 percent of pull requests.
GitHub keynote speaker Jay Parikh argued that useful output is completed outcomes rather than more code.
Parikh defined completed outcomes as shipping features, fixing software bugs, and addressing security issues.
Current industry business models charge cloud customers on a consumption basis rather than an outcome basis.
Presently, nothing across the enterprise technology landscape ties AI spending directly to worthwhile output.
AWS continues to bill external Bedrock and Kiro clients directly by the consumed token.

### What to watch next

Workers are waiting to see if promotion documents can survive citing AI non-use as a valid finding.
Industry watchers question whether performance decisions will reward long-term outcomes over tool activity.
Engineers will monitor how managers handle justified decisions to avoid using automated tools.
The connection between AI policy enforcement and future Amazon staff cuts remains under close scrutiny.

Background:

- Software developers frequently express concerns about automation replacing traditional engineering roles.
- Consumption-based pricing remains the dominant revenue model for major cloud infrastructure providers.
